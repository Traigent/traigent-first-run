The close reads a flat comparison one way only. Its hypothesis list already
names easy data, and every remedy under it looks upward - verify the references,
add a structural knob, then consider a separately disclosed stronger model. That
is the right answer when the level is low and the wrong one when the level is
high, and nothing in the guide said so, so the run that found every
configuration scoring near the top of its own metric was pointed at more
capability and more spend.

What these bytes buy is the opposite escalation, stated where the first one
lives so a reader meets both together. When the paired analysis detects no
difference between configurations and the level is high, nothing the run tried
separated them because these configurations are not what limits the result. The
cheapest point measured is then the answer rather than one option on a frontier,
and the next experiment worth running is a tier below anything tested. A reader
who has just been told to look harder will not derive that from a list of
candidate causes.

It is written as a reading and never as a recommendation to switch, because the
bound it sits under is not negotiable: this run tested a handful of
configurations out of a space larger than its trial cap, so a cheaper tier
nobody ran is a hypothesis to carry to full scale exactly as a cheaper tier that
did run and matched already is. It earns no further paid round here.

The collected-data condition is the other half and is the failure this could
otherwise cause. On generated rows a high flat score measures material the run
invented, and an easy-looking task is the likeliest thing a walkthrough dataset
produces - so recommending a cheaper model on that evidence would be the
synthetic-versus-real confusion the whole guide is built to prevent, arriving
through a cost saving instead of an accuracy claim.
